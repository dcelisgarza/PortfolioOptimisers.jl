Log-wealth regret

log_wealth_regret compares two prediction results scored over the same timestamps, a strategy and a comparator. It returns the difference in log final wealth between the comparator and the strategy, which is positive when the comparator ends with more. It also returns the difference at each row, and a Diebold-Mariano-West test that the two have the same expected log growth, WildBootstrapMeanTest by default, in the same form as covariance_forecast_compare. It was written for the online selection rules, and it accepts any two prediction results over one sequence of rows. Called with the strategy alone, it compares the strategy against the benchmark of its own rows, the B of the returns data, in batch and online alike.

The comparator can be the same estimator run causally. It can also be a hindsight comparator, an estimator fitted on the rows it is scored on and predicted on those same rows. BestConstantRebalancedPortfolio gives the best constant rebalanced portfolio. A ScoreSelector that keeps the top asset, composed with EqualWeighted, gives the best single asset.

Run through a HindsightSplit, the same estimators give the comparators of dynamic regret, which can change at each row. These are be-the-leader, the best constant rebalanced portfolio over the rows up to and including each row, and the best portfolio of each period. The result reports the path length of these comparators next to the regret. BudgetedHindsightPath is the third comparator of dynamic regret, the best sequence of weights whose path length stays within a budget. Like the others, it is an estimator. Its fit is one optimisation over the whole panel, and its prediction result has one fold per row.

fold_target and fold_held read one fold of a prediction result. They give the weights that the fold started from and the weights that it held after its last observation, on the names rd.nx of the fold. When the fit drops an asset that it cannot hold, res.w has fewer entries than rd.nx. These two readers give one entry for each name of rd.nx.

struct LogWealthRegretResult{__T_regret, __T_regret_per_period, __T_difference, __T_cumulative, __T_t_stat, __T_p_value, __T_mean_ci_lower, __T_mean_ci_upper, __T_n_periods, __T_wealth_a, __T_wealth_b, __T_path_length} <: AbstractResult

Holds the log-wealth regret of a strategy against a comparator, and the test of its per-row difference.

log_wealth_regret returns it, over one sequence of rows, one for each member when a prediction holds many members. The regret is the comparator's log terminal wealth less the strategy's, so it is positive when the comparator wins. The per-row difference carries a Mean Test of equal expected log growth, in the shape of CovarianceForecastComparisonResult. A negative regret is common and is not a defect: a causal strategy that reads the market's structure can beat a constant portfolio, and a Hindsight Comparator is best only over the class it was chosen from.

Two fields state the comparator's side of a dynamic-regret bound. path_length is $P_T$, the summed Euclidean distance between the comparator's consecutive targets: zero for a constant comparator, and larger with every switch that a HindsightSplit comparator makes. cumulative is the running regret.

  • difference: Per-row series $\log(1 + r_{b,t}) - \log(1 + r_{a,t})$, the comparator's log growth less the strategy's at every period.
  • cumulative: Running regret, the cumulative sum of difference: its last entry is regret.

The log-wealth regret of strategy a against comparator b over one and the same sequence of rows. With no b, the comparator is the benchmark of a's own rows.

A prediction whose optimiser gives many portfolios, such as a frontier sweep, holds one series for each member. The verb then gives one LogWealthRegretResult for each member, in a vector, and member_regret pairs the members. Two sides with many members pair by index, so they must hold the same count. A side with one series pairs with every member of the other side. The benchmark of a has the shape of its series, so each member of a meets its own benchmark. The shape of the answer follows the types of the series, never their count: a one-series pair gives one Result, and every other pair gives a vector.

Regret is defined over one sequence, so the verb refuses two prediction results that do not carry the same timestamps. Each series is read as it was scored: net of the fee the fold settled, and drifted where a Weight Drift ran. The comparator's fee policy is therefore the caller's, and a fee-free comparator against a fee-paying strategy measures the fee as regret.

The benchmark comparator is the series that regret_benchmark reads off a, in batch and through an online walk-forward alike. A benchmark matrix was collapsed by each fold's weights, so it is one series too. The benchmark pays no fee, so the regret against it is the negated excess log wealth of the strategy net of its fees. It holds no weights, so its path length is NaN.

The comparator is any prediction result over the rows. It can be the same estimator run causally through cross_val_predict with the strategy's cv. It can also be a Hindsight Comparator, an estimator fit on the rows it is scored on and predicted over them, predict(optimise(est, rd_test), rd_test). Two Hindsight Comparators are common:

  • The best constant rebalanced portfolio in hindsight: BestConstantRebalancedPortfolio with no solver, or MeanRisk under LogarithmicReturn and MaximumReturn with a solver, which is exact under any bound.
  • The best stock in hindsight: a ScoreSelector under RankRule(; best = 1) and MeanReturn(; flag = true), composed with EqualWeighted in a Pipeline.

No scorer and no summary column ship for regret. A hyperparameter search ranks on MeanReturn(; flag = true), the log wealth per period, which orders candidates as the regret against any fixed comparator orders them. The performance summary reads one series and holds no comparator.

Mathematical definition

\[\begin{align} \delta_t &= \log\left(1 + r_{b,t}\right) - \log\left(1 + r_{a,t}\right)\,, \\ R &= \sum_{t=1}^{T} \delta_t\,, \quad \bar{\delta} = \frac{R}{T}\,, \\ t_{\delta} &= \frac{\bar{\delta}}{s_{\delta}}\,, \\ P_T &= \sum_{t=2}^{T} \lVert \boldsymbol{u}_t - \boldsymbol{u}_{t-1} \rVert_2\,. \end{align}\]

Where:

  • $r_{a,t}$, $r_{b,t}$: Realised return of the strategy and of the comparator at period $t$, as scored.
  • $\delta_t$: Per-row difference in log growth.
  • $R$: Log-wealth regret, positive when the comparator wins.
  • $\bar{\delta}$: Regret per period.
  • $T$: Number of periods.
  • $s_{\delta}$: Long-run standard error of $\bar{\delta}$, which the Mean Test mt defines.
  • $t_{\delta}$: t-statistic of the regret per period. The p-value and the interval of mt read it.
  • $P_T = \sum_{t=2}^{T} \lVert \boldsymbol{u}_t - \boldsymbol{u}_{t-1} \rVert_2$: Path length of a comparator sequence $\boldsymbol{u}_1, \ldots, \boldsymbol{u}_T$ in the feasible set.
  • $\boldsymbol{u}_t$: Comparator's target on fold $t$, embedded by asset name on the union of the folds' universes.

$R$ is the logarithm of the ratio of the two wealths. Against the best constant rebalanced portfolio in hindsight, $-\bar{\delta}$ is the quantity of Equation 1.7 of [7], and that equation states that it tends to zero for the universal portfolio. Equation 2 of [5] names $R$ the regret. $t_{\delta}$ is the statistic of the test of equal expected loss of Section 1.1 of [225], on the loss $-\log(1 + r_t)$. covariance_forecast_compare states why the default WildBootstrapMeanTest reads it, and not a fixed number of lags: the log growths of two strategies cluster with the volatility, so their difference can be correlated over many rows. Each row is one entry of the series, so the test reads a window of one, as loss_difference_test states. OverlapMeanTest gives the statistic of the rule of zero lags.

Under equal expected log growth, the law that mt reads is the null law of $t_{\delta}$ when the estimates that the strategy reads do not move its variance. Theorem 4.1(a) of [226] states two such cases: the periods are few next to the rows of the first training window, or the expected gradient of the difference in the estimates is zero. Otherwise Theorem 4.1(b) of [226] adds terms to the variance, and $s_{\delta}$ omits them. The test is valid only for a comparator that did not read the rows it is scored on. Against a Hindsight Comparator it is optimistic by construction: the comparator was chosen on the sequence that the difference is tested over, so p_value overstates the evidence that the comparator is better.

Four degenerate series have a stated answer:

  • Two identical series have $\bar{\delta} = 0$ and no long-run variance, so the t-statistic, the p-value and the interval are NaN.
  • A difference that is a non-zero constant has no long-run variance. In exact arithmetic the test answers NaN. In floating point the round-off of the difference can be larger than the spread that the Mean Test reads as a constant series. Then the t-statistic is a very large finite number, and the p-value is the least that the test gives, 1 / (n_sim + 1) under WildBootstrapMeanTest.
  • A return of $-1$ is a total loss. Its log growth is $-\infty$, so $R$ is infinite, and the test is NaN.
  • A NaN return makes $R$ and the test NaN.

Dynamic regret is regret against a comparator that moves. Definition 7 of [227] states it, and Theorem 2 of [227] bounds it in the path length of Definition 6. Its bounds are stated in $P_T$, so $R$ and $P_T$ are read together: a static comparator has $P_T = 0$ and its regret is the static regret, and a moving comparator buys its lower regret with a path that the bound charges for. $P_T$ is NaN when the comparator is one prediction result, which holds one target and no path, or the benchmark, which holds no weights. The literature states dynamic regret against three comparators, and each is the same estimator through its own scheme. On the three-row example $\boldsymbol{x}_1 = (1.2, 0.98)$, $\boldsymbol{x}_2 = (0.9, 1.1)$, $\boldsymbol{x}_3 = (1.3, 0.7)$ of price relatives:

  • Static: the best constant rebalanced portfolio over all three rows, predict(optimise(est, rd), rd), is all-in on the first asset. Its wealth is $1.2 \cdot 0.9 \cdot 1.3 = 1.404$, and $P_T = 0$. est is BestConstantRebalancedPortfolio, or MeanRisk under LogarithmicReturn and MaximumReturn.
  • Be-the-leader: cross_val_predict(est, rd, HindsightSplit()) plays on row $t$ the best constant rebalanced portfolio over the rows through $t$. The targets are $\boldsymbol{u}_1 = (1, 0)$, $\boldsymbol{u}_2 = (23/44, 21/44) \approx (0.523, 0.477)$ and $\boldsymbol{u}_3 = (1, 0)$. The wealth is $1.2 \cdot 0.995 \cdot 1.3 \approx 1.553$, and $P_T = 2 \sqrt{2} \cdot 21/44 \approx 1.350$. The first fold trains on one row, so MeanRisk needs a prior that fits one row, which HindsightSplit states.
  • Per-period minimiser: the top-1 ScoreSelector under MeanReturn(; flag = true), composed with EqualWeighted through HindsightSplit(; prefix = false), is one-hot on each row's best asset. Its wealth is $1.2 \cdot 1.1 \cdot 1.3 = 1.716$, and $P_T = 2 \sqrt{2}$.

The bound also reads the other way. The regret at a given budget $L$ is the gap to the best sequence whose path length is at most $L$. Neither be-the-leader nor the per-period minimiser is that sequence. BudgetedHindsightPath fits it, and its prediction result has one fold per row for this verb to read.

Algorithm

  1. Read the strategy's series and timestamps through regret_series, giving ra and tsa.
  2. With b, read the comparator's series and timestamps the same way, giving rb and tsb, and check that the two sides share their timestamps. With no b, read the benchmark of a through regret_benchmark, giving rb, and check that it exists.
  3. Pass ra, rb, mt and the comparator to member_regret. It pairs the members, and it gives each pair to series_regret, which forms the regret, its test and the path length of the comparator's member.

Arguments

  • a: The strategy's prediction result.
  • b: The comparator's prediction result. Leave it out to compare against the benchmark of a.
  • mt: The Mean Test of the per-row difference.

Validation

  • a and b carry the same timestamps, and each pair of series has the same number of rows. An ArgumentError is thrown otherwise: two runs over different rows compare nothing.
  • When a and b both hold many members, they hold the same count of members. A DimensionMismatch that names both counts is thrown otherwise, because no pairing by index exists.
  • With no b, a carries a benchmark. An ArgumentError is thrown otherwise, because there is no comparator.
  • No return of either series is below $-1$. A DomainError is thrown otherwise, because a negative wealth has no log. A NaN return passes, and its NaN reaches the regret.

Returns

  • reg::LogWealthRegretResult: When each side holds one series. The regret, its per-period form, the per-row difference, its running sum and its test, the two terminal wealths, and the comparator's path length.
  • reg::Vector{<:LogWealthRegretResult}: When a side holds many members. Entry j is the regret of member j.

PortfolioOptimisers.BudgetedHindsightPath — Type
struct BudgetedHindsightPath{__T_L, __T_p, __T_wb, __T_sets, __T_strict, __T_slv, __T_fb} <: OptimisationEstimator

Fits the path of per-row allocations with the largest log wealth whose summed step length is at most L.

Dynamic regret at a budget is the gap to this path. At p = 2 this path is the comparator of Definition 7 of [227], at the cost $c_t(\boldsymbol{u}) = -\log(\boldsymbol{u} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_t)$. Definition 6 of [227] measures the path in the Euclidean distance, so at any other p the budget bounds a different length. Neither per-row comparator that a HindsightSplit builds is this path: be-the-leader is one point at its own path length, and the per-period minimiser is the limit with no budget. The fit is one concave programme over the whole panel. The Lagrangian form, which puts a penalty on the path length in place of the budget, is the same programme with one term moved, and the library does not build it.

The estimator follows the Hindsight Comparator rule of log_wealth_regret, as every other comparator does. optimise returns a BudgetedHindsightPathResult, which holds the path. predict over the same rows returns one fold per row, and it refuses any other rows. So predict(optimise(est, rd_test), rd_test) is a prediction result that the regret verb reads unchanged, and the verb reports its Euclidean path length beside the regret.

Each row's universe is the Coverage Universe of its one-row window, as coverage_mask derives it: an asset is in the row when its return is finite and the Asset Panel's active mask is true there. A static panel, or no panel, reads finiteness alone. A HindsightSplit(; prefix = false) fold fits on the same universe, so the two per-row comparators agree on what a row can hold. The result carries the masks, so predict views each row at its own mask and names no Held Gap. A row with no asset in its universe is refused, because its budget of one cannot be met.

The budget is stated in the norm of order p, but LogWealthRegretResult reports the path length in the Euclidean norm. The two numbers agree at p = 2 alone. A tight budget on a long panel puts most step cones at their apex, where an interior-point solver can stall short of its tolerance and report insufficient progress. A first-order solver solves such a programme, so on a panel of many rows the robust slv is a solver vector with a first-order solver as the fallback. The fit reads the rows it is scored on, so the test of log_wealth_regret against it is optimistic by construction.

Mathematical definition

\[\begin{align} \underset{\boldsymbol{u}_1, \ldots, \boldsymbol{u}_T}{\max} &\quad \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}_t} u_{t,i}\, x_{t,i} \\ \textrm{s.t.} &\quad \sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}_t} u_{t,i} = 1\,, \quad l_i \leq u_{t,i} \leq h_i \;\; \forall i \in \mathcal{M}_t\,, \quad u_{t,i} = 0 \;\; \forall i \notin \mathcal{M}_t\,, \\ &\quad \sum_{t=2}^{T} \lVert \boldsymbol{u}_t - \boldsymbol{u}_{t-1} \rVert_p \leq L\,. \end{align}\]

Where:

  • $\boldsymbol{u}_t$: Comparator's allocation on row $t$, with entries $u_{t,i}$.
  • $\boldsymbol{x}_t$: Price relative vector of period $t$, each asset's price over its price one period earlier.
  • $x_{t,i}$: Entry $i$ of $\boldsymbol{x}_t$, one plus the return of asset $i$ on row $t$.
  • $\mathcal{M}_t$: Coverage Universe of row $t$, the assets that the row can hold.
  • $l_i$, $h_i$: Resolved lower and upper weight bounds of asset $i$, zero and one by default.
  • $L$: Path-length budget.
  • $p$: Norm order.
  • $T$: Number of observations.

Three consequences follow from the programme:

  • At $L = 0$ the path is constant. It holds only the assets that every row covers, and it is the best constant rebalanced portfolio in hindsight over them, as BestConstantRebalancedPortfolio fits it under the same bounds. When no asset is in every row's universe, the programme is infeasible.
  • When $L$ is at least the path length of the per-period minimiser, the budget is slack. Under the default bounds the answer is then that minimiser, one-hot on each row's best asset, when each row has one best asset.
  • An asset that delists inside the panel is outside every later row's universe. The path therefore sells it on its last row at any budget, and the forced step counts towards the path length like any other step.

Between the two ends the path spends the budget where a switch buys the most log wealth, and the regret against it is the regret at that budget.

Fields

  • L: The path-length budget, 0 <= L.
  • p: The norm order of the budget, 1 <= p.
  • wb: Weight bounds.
  • sets: Sets used to map estimator values to assets.
  • strict: Whether a constraint that names an asset or a group that sets does not hold raises an error. When it is false, the constraint generator warns and skips the entry.
  • slv: Solver or vector of solvers.
  • fb: Fallback estimator, another BudgetedHindsightPath that optimise runs when the fit fails, or nothing.

PortfolioOptimisers._optimise — Method
_optimise(est::BudgetedHindsightPath, rd::ReturnsResult; kwargs...) -> BudgetedHindsightPathResult

Solves the budgeted path programme of BudgetedHindsightPath over the rows of rd.

Every call reaches this method through optimise, which walks est.fb on a failure. The value of a variable fixed at zero is the solver's zero, so the fit writes an exact zero at every entry outside a row's mask.

Algorithm

  1. Check that rd.X is not nothing, and that est.sets names the columns of rd, in order, through assert_universe_axis_order.
  2. Add one to the returns, giving the price relatives X, of size T × N.
  3. Derive the rows' Investable Masks through path_row_masks, giving imsk, and msk, which is all true when imsk is nothing.
  4. Resolve the weight bounds through weight_bounds_constraints, giving wb.
  5. Make a bare model with the allocation variables u and the log-wealth variables z.
  6. Write each row through path_row_constraints!, and the budget through path_budget_constraint!.
  7. Set the objective, and solve the model through optimise_JuMP_model!, giving res.
  8. Read the path W from u and OptimisationSuccess on a success. On a failure, fill W with NaN and make an OptimisationFailure that names the trials.
  9. Set every entry of W outside msk to zero.

JuMP formulation

Variables

  • u: created, an anonymous T × N matrix of allocations, one row per period.
  • z: created, an anonymous vector of $T$ log-wealth variables, one per period.

Constraints

The rows of path_row_constraints!, one call per period, which give $z_t \leq \log \sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}_t} u_{t,i}\, x_{t,i}$, and the rows of path_budget_constraint!. The function registers no row itself.

Objective

  • Max: $\sum_{t=1}^{T} z_t$, the log wealth of the path.

Where:

  • $z_t$: Log-wealth variable of period $t$.
  • $u_{t,i}$: Allocation of asset $i$ in period $t$.
  • $x_{t,i}$: Price relative of asset $i$ in period $t$, one plus its return.
  • $\mathcal{M}_t$: Coverage Universe of period $t$.
  • $T$: Number of observations.

Arguments

  • est: The estimator.
  • rd: The returns result to use. Its returns matrix is the panel the path is solved over.
  • kwargs: Ignored.

Validation

  • !isnothing(rd.X). An IsNothingError is thrown otherwise.
  • The rules of assert_universe_axis_order.
  • Every row has at least one asset in its Coverage Universe, through path_row_masks.

Returns

  • res::BudgetedHindsightPathResult: The path, or the failure.

StatsAPI.predict — Method
predict(res::BudgetedHindsightPathResult, rd::ReturnsResult) -> MultiPeriodPredictionResult

The prediction result of a budgeted path over the rows it was solved on: one fold per row, each a PredictionResult whose NaiveOptimisationResult holds that row's allocation on the resolved bounds, with the row's Investable Mask when the row has a missing return, predicted over that row alone.

The path is bound to its rows, so rd must be the panel the path was solved over: the same names, the same clock and the same row count. predict(optimise(est, rd_test), rd_test) is then the Hindsight Comparator rule of log_wealth_regret, and the regret verb reads the folds unchanged.

Arguments

  • res: The fit.
  • rd: The returns result to use. The panel the path was solved over.

Validation

  • rd carries the names, the clock and the row count of res. An ArgumentError is thrown otherwise: a path over other rows is another fit.

Returns

  • pred::MultiPeriodPredictionResult: One fold per row.

PortfolioOptimisers.fold_target — Function
fold_target(pred::PredictionResult, j::Integer = 1)

The weights a fold started from, and, through fold_held, the weights it held after its last observation, on the fold's own asset names.

On a fold with no Held Weights record, fold_target is the fold's target. On a fold with one, it is the record's w0, the start that held_start_weights names: the fold's own target when it solved, and the weights it was handed when it failed. fold_held is the target on a fold with no record, because a fold without a drift holds its target to the end. On a fold with a record it is the record's w, the drifted holding that the next fold trades from.

Both read member j through member_vector, when the optimiser of the fold gives many portfolios. The default is the first member, which held_path_turnover reads. Both are viewed at the fold's Investable Mask, so they sit on pred.rd.nx, the names of the fold's own universe, which stacked_fold_weights embeds by.

Arguments

  • pred: One fold's prediction result.
  • j: The index of the member.

Returns

  • w::VecNum: The weights, one entry per name in pred.rd.nx.
