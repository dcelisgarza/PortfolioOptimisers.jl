Risk measure whose value is the expected portfolio return of a prior result.

ExpectedReturn is a NonOptimisationRiskMeasure, so an optimiser cannot use it as an objective or as a constraint. Summarise a realised return series as a PerformanceSummaryResult.

The methods that take weights and returns first make the net returns with calc_net_returns. So the summary of a portfolio includes its fees.

Every method takes a benchmark, a second return series over the same periods, and gives the three excess statistics against it. When no benchmark is given, the three are NaN. On the prediction-result method, benchmark defaults to the benchmark of the rows that the prediction scored, which regret_benchmark reads. So a prediction over returns data with a B gives the three statistics with no keyword, and benchmark = nothing turns them off. The other methods hold no prediction, and they read no B.

Each fold collapsed a benchmark matrix with its own weights, as collapse_benchmark states. Against such a benchmark, excess_ret is the return of $(\boldsymbol{X} - \boldsymbol{B}) \boldsymbol{w}$. That is the active return against the same weights in the benchmark assets, and not the excess over one index.

The prediction-result method also gives the turnover of the held path, the mean trade at a rebalance of a multi-period result. At test_size = 1, a rebalance is a period. A bare series, a single fold and a result of one fold record no rebalance, so their turnover is NaN.

The series ret must be finite, as the Precomputed-returns contract states. No method checks that the series is finite, because every internal caller gives a finite series. The prediction methods read the output of calc_net_returns for the fold, and a check of a long series costs time on every call. One entry that is not finite makes the mean, the volatility and every ratio not finite. The tail figure then gives a finite wrong number and not a NaN, because partialsort puts a NaN after every real number. A caller with a gapped series must first remove the gaps with x[isfinite.(x)]. A caller with a gapped panel must score it through predict(res::NonFiniteAllocationOptimisationResult, rd::ReturnsResult), which filters the Held Gaps once.

The standard error of the Sharpe ratio reads every observation as independent. So on a series scored under a Weight Drift, it is less than the true standard error, as the # Mathematical definition states. A long-run variance estimator corrects for serial dependence, but it needs a bandwidth, and no bandwidth is correct for every sample. The library does not build one. It applies no guard and no threshold, and it gives the figure with no change.

Mathematical definition

The five headline statistics:

\[\begin{align} \mathrm{ann\_return} &= m p\\ \mathrm{ann\_volatility} &= s \sqrt{p}\\ \mathrm{sharpe} &= \dfrac{\mathrm{ann\_return}}{\mathrm{ann\_volatility}}\\ \mathrm{sortino} &= \dfrac{\mathrm{ann\_return}}{\sqrt{\dfrac{p}{T} \sum\limits_{t=1}^{T} \min(x_{t}, 0)^{2}}}\\ \mathrm{calmar} &= \dfrac{\mathrm{ann\_return}}{\left\lvert \min_{t} d_{t} \right\rvert}\,. \end{align}\]

The three excess statistics against a benchmark, and the turnover of the held path of a multi-period result:

\[\begin{align} \mathrm{excess\_ret} &= m_e p\\ \mathrm{tracking\_error} &= s_e \sqrt{p}\\ \mathrm{information\_ratio} &= \dfrac{\mathrm{excess\_ret}}{\mathrm{tracking\_error}}\\ \mathrm{turnover} &= \dfrac{1}{F - 1} \sum_{f=2}^{F} \left\lVert \boldsymbol{w}_f - \hat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{f-1} \right\rVert_1\,. \end{align}\]

The standard error of the Sharpe ratio corrects for the skewness and the excess kurtosis of the returns. Its variance is the asymptotic variance of Equation 2 of [287], divided by $T - 1$ as in Section 2.5 of [288], and annualised:

\[\begin{align} \mathrm{sharpe\_stderr} &= \sqrt{\dfrac{p}{T - 1} \left(1 - g_{1} \dfrac{m}{s} + \dfrac{g_{2} + 2}{4} \dfrac{m^{2}}{s^{2}}\right)}\,. \end{align}\]

Where:

  • $\boldsymbol{x}$: Portfolio returns vector $T \times 1$.
  • $\boldsymbol{b}$: Benchmark vector $T \times 1$.
  • $T$: Number of observations.
  • $p$: Number of periods per year, ppy.
  • $m$, $s$: Sample mean and corrected sample standard deviation of $\boldsymbol{x}$.
  • $\boldsymbol{d}$: Drawdown path of the cumulative wealth of $\boldsymbol{x}$, compounded or not as compound states, with $d_{t} \leq 0$.
  • $m_e$, $s_e$: Sample mean and corrected sample standard deviation of the excess series $\boldsymbol{e} = \boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{b}$.
  • $F$: Number of folds of the multi-period result.
  • $\boldsymbol{w}_f$: Weights at the start of fold $f$, its target on a solved fold.
  • $\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}_f$: Weights of fold $f$ after its last observation. They are the drifted holding when the fold has a Held Weights record, and $\boldsymbol{w}_f$ otherwise.
  • $g_{1}$, $g_{2}$: Sample skewness and sample excess kurtosis of $\boldsymbol{x}$.

The turnover puts every weight vector on the union of the universes of the folds, by asset name. A weight is zero at an asset that its fold does not hold. Each term of the sum is the trade at one rebalance. calc_turnover reads that quantity at one step, and Turnover bounds it. The drift in a fold is not a trade, so it enters the turnover only through $\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{f-1}$.

The downside deviation of the Sortino ratio divides by $T$, and not by the number of periods with a loss. This is the convention of Sortino and Price. With it, the ratios of two series with different frequencies of loss stay comparable.

The kurtosis term is $(g_{2} + 2)/4$. Section 2.5 of [288] states it as $(\gamma_{4} - 1)/4$ on the raw fourth standardised moment $\gamma_{4}$, and StatsBase.kurtosis gives the excess moment $g_{2} = \gamma_{4} - 3$. The two forms are equal. Only this form becomes the naive $\sqrt{(1 + \mathrm{SR}^{2}/2)/T}$ on a normal series. Over 200,000 samples of 250 normal returns at a Sharpe ratio per period of 0.5, the sample standard deviation of the Sharpe ratio is 0.06728323. This expression at the population moments gives 0.06721661, and the same expression without the $+2$ gives 0.06337243.

A return series that is not normal makes a Sharpe ratio less precise than the naive expression shows, and a negative skew makes it less precise again. A real portfolio has such a series, so the summary gives the standard error with the ratio.

The expression corrects for the third and fourth moments and not for serial dependence, so it reads every observation as independent of the others. A series scored under a Weight Drift is serially dependent through the weights that it held, because a position that grew has a larger weight in the next observation. So on such a series, this figure is less than the true standard error.

Algorithm

Every method calls summarise_returns, which does the steps. A method that takes weights first makes the net returns with calc_net_returns. The method that takes a prediction result reads the first series when it holds many series. It sets the default benchmark to the first series of its own B, and it reads the turnover of its held path.

  1. Check alpha and ppy, as # Validation states.
  2. Take T, the number of periods. Take m, the sample mean of ret, and clamp it to the least and the greatest entry of ret. Take s, the corrected sample standard deviation of ret about m. The clamp changes nothing in exact arithmetic. The rounded mean of a constant ret can differ from its common value, and the clamp makes m equal to that value, so s is exactly zero.
  3. Annualise m and s, to get ann_ret and ann_vol.
  4. Divide ann_ret by ann_vol, to get sharpe. When ann_vol is not positive, sharpe is NaN.
  5. Set every positive entry of ret to zero. Square the entries, average them over all T periods, annualise, and take the square root, to get the downside deviation ddev. Divide ann_ret by ddev, to get sortino. When ddev is not positive, sortino is NaN.
  6. Build the cumulative wealth series with cumulative_returns, and take its drawdown path with drawdowns. Read max_dd, the minimum of that path. Divide ann_ret by the absolute value of max_dd, to get calmar. When max_dd is not negative, calmar is NaN.
  7. Compute ConditionalValueatRisk at alpha on ret, and change its sign, to get cvar_val in return space.
  8. Make the Sharpe ratio per period sr_p. Correct its variance var_sr for the sample skewness and the sample excess kurtosis of ret. Annualise the square root, to get sharpe_se. When var_sr is not positive, sharpe_se is NaN.
  9. Compute the three excess statistics with excess_statistics. With a benchmark, the function checks its length and takes e = ret - benchmark. It annualises the mean of e, clamped as in step 2, and the standard deviation of e about that mean, to get excess_ret and tracking_error. It divides them, to get information_ratio, which is NaN when tracking_error is not positive. With no benchmark, all three are NaN.
  10. Take the turnover that the prediction-result method computed with held_path_turnover. A nothing from that function, and every other method, gives a NaN.
  11. Put the four inputs and the twelve statistics into a PerformanceSummaryResult.

Arguments

  • ret: Vector of periodic portfolio returns.
  • w: Portfolio weights.
  • X: Data matrix observations × assets if the dims keyword does not exist or dims = 1, assets × observations when dims = 2.
  • fees: Optional transaction fees.
  • rd: ReturnsResult that holds the asset returns.
  • res: An OptimisationResult. result_investable_view puts its weights and fees, and the rd of the caller, on the investable universe of the result.
  • ppy: Periods per year, the annualisation factor. 252 for daily, 52 for weekly, 12 for monthly returns, and 1 states each figure per period. It defaults to 1, which gives the statistics per period at any frequency of the data.
  • alpha: Tail probability of the CVaR, $\alpha \in (0, 1)$.
  • compound: true when the drawdown statistics read a compounded wealth path, and false when they read the cumulative sum of the returns.
  • benchmark: Benchmark return series over the same periods as ret, or nothing for no excess statistics. It defaults to nothing, and on a prediction result to the benchmark of its rows.

Validation

  • 0 < alpha < 1.
  • ppy > 0.
  • length(benchmark) == length(ret) when a benchmark is given. Otherwise the function raises a DimensionMismatch.

PortfolioOptimisers.probabilistic_sharpe_ratio — Function
probabilistic_sharpe_ratio(x::VecNum; bsr::Number = 0, rf::Number = 0)
probabilistic_sharpe_ratio(X::MatNum; bsr::Number = 0, rf::Number = 0)

Compute the Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio, the probability that the true Sharpe ratio of a return series is greater than a benchmark.

The Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio of [288] corrects the sample Sharpe ratio for the length of the series and for the skewness and the kurtosis of its returns. Fat tails make a Sharpe ratio that is not zero less precise, and negative skewness makes a positive Sharpe ratio less precise. When the Sharpe ratio is above the benchmark, a less precise Sharpe ratio has a smaller probability. Every quantity is per period, so x, bsr and rf must have the same frequency, and the function annualises none of them. The matrix method computes one probability for each column.

Mathematical definition

\[\begin{align} \mathrm{PSR}\left(\mathrm{SR}^{*}\right) &= \Phi\left(\dfrac{\left(\widehat{\mathrm{SR}} - \mathrm{SR}^{*}\right) \sqrt{T - 1}}{\sqrt{1 - \hat{\gamma}_{3} \widehat{\mathrm{SR}} + \dfrac{\hat{\gamma}_{4} - 1}{4} \widehat{\mathrm{SR}}^{2}}}\right)\,. \end{align}\]

Where:

  • $\mathrm{PSR}$: Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio.
  • $\mathrm{SR}^{*}$: Benchmark Sharpe ratio per period.
  • $\Phi$: Distribution function of the standard normal distribution, and $\Phi^{-1}$ its quantile function.
  • $\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}$: Sample Sharpe ratio per period of $\boldsymbol{x}$, the excess of its sample mean over the risk-free rate rf divided by its corrected sample standard deviation.
  • $T$: Number of observations.
  • $\hat{\gamma}_{3}$: Sample skewness of $\boldsymbol{x}$, its third central moment divided by the cube of its biased sample standard deviation.
  • $\hat{\gamma}_{4}$: Sample kurtosis of $\boldsymbol{x}$, its fourth central moment divided by the square of its biased sample variance. It is $3$ for a normal distribution.
  • $\boldsymbol{x}$: Portfolio returns vector $T \times 1$.

The denominator is the square root of the variance factor of sharpe_ratio_variance_factor. A Sharpe ratio equal to the benchmark gives a probability of $1/2$.

Algorithm

  1. Check bsr and rf, as # Validation states.
  2. Take T, sr and v, the number of observations, the sample Sharpe ratio and its variance factor, through sharpe_ratio_moments. It drops the NaN entries of x.
  3. Multiply the excess sr - bsr by the square root of T - 1, and divide it by the square root of v, to get z.
  4. Evaluate the standard normal distribution function at z. A NaN from step 2 gives a NaN.

Arguments

  • x: Periodic return series, or a matrix with one return series in each column. A NaN entry is an observation that is not available, and the function drops it.
  • bsr: Benchmark Sharpe ratio per period, in the frequency of x. It defaults to 0.
  • rf: Risk-free rate. It defaults to 0, in the frequency of x.

Validation

  • isfinite(bsr).

Compute the Minimum Track Record Length, the number of observations that a return series needs for its Sharpe ratio to exceed a benchmark at a confidence level.

The Minimum Track Record Length of [288] is the least number of observations at which the probabilistic_sharpe_ratio of the series reaches 1 - alpha, for the sample moments of the series. It is a number of observations, not a number of years. The matrix method computes one length for each column.

Mathematical definition

\[\begin{align} \mathrm{MinTRL} &= 1 + \left(1 - \hat{\gamma}_{3} \widehat{\mathrm{SR}} + \dfrac{\hat{\gamma}_{4} - 1}{4} \widehat{\mathrm{SR}}^{2}\right) \left(\dfrac{\Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha)}{\widehat{\mathrm{SR}} - \mathrm{SR}^{*}}\right)^{2}\,. \end{align}\]

Where:

  • $\mathrm{MinTRL}$: Minimum Track Record Length, a number of observations.
  • $\alpha$: Significance level, so $1 - \alpha$ is the confidence level.
  • $\hat{\gamma}_{3}$: Sample skewness of $\boldsymbol{x}$, its third central moment divided by the cube of its biased sample standard deviation.
  • $\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}$: Sample Sharpe ratio per period of $\boldsymbol{x}$, the excess of its sample mean over the risk-free rate rf divided by its corrected sample standard deviation.
  • $\hat{\gamma}_{4}$: Sample kurtosis of $\boldsymbol{x}$, its fourth central moment divided by the square of its biased sample variance. It is $3$ for a normal distribution.
  • $\Phi$: Distribution function of the standard normal distribution, and $\Phi^{-1}$ its quantile function.
  • $\mathrm{SR}^{*}$: Benchmark Sharpe ratio per period.
  • $\boldsymbol{x}$: Portfolio returns vector $T \times 1$.

The length solves $\mathrm{PSR}(\mathrm{SR}^{*}) = 1 - \alpha$ for $T$. When $\widehat{\mathrm{SR}} \leq \mathrm{SR}^{*}$, the Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio is at most $1/2$ for every $T$, so no length is enough and the length is $+\infty$.

Algorithm

  1. Check alpha, bsr and rf, as # Validation states.
  2. Take sr and v, the sample Sharpe ratio and its variance factor, through sharpe_ratio_moments. It drops the NaN entries of x.
  3. Take the excess d = sr - bsr, and za, the quantile of the standard normal distribution at 1 - alpha.
  4. Add one to v times the square of za / d, to get n.
  5. When d is not positive and n is not NaN, replace n with Inf.

Arguments

  • x: Periodic return series, or a matrix with one return series in each column. A NaN entry is an observation that is not available, and the function drops it.
  • bsr: Benchmark Sharpe ratio per period, in the frequency of x. It defaults to 0.
  • rf: Risk-free rate. It defaults to 0, in the frequency of x.
  • alpha: Significance level. It defaults to 0.05, a confidence level of 95 %.

Validation

  • isfinite(rf).

  • n::Number: The length, for a vector x. It is Inf when the sample Sharpe ratio does not exceed bsr. It is NaN in the cases where probabilistic_sharpe_ratio is NaN.

Approximate the expected maximum of the Sharpe ratios of N independent trials.

The best of several trials has a Sharpe ratio that the selection itself inflates. The approximation of [289] reads the trial Sharpe ratios as independent draws of one normal distribution. deflated_sharpe_ratio uses it at mu = 0 as the benchmark that the selected trial must exceed. Trials with similar parameters have correlated Sharpe ratios, and then implied_independent_trials gives a smaller N.

Mathematical definition

\[\begin{align} \mathbb{E}\left[\max_{n} \widehat{\mathrm{SR}}_{n}\right] &\approx \mu + \sigma \max\left(0,\, (1 - \gamma) \Phi^{-1}\left(1 - \dfrac{1}{N}\right) + \gamma \Phi^{-1}\left(1 - \dfrac{1}{N e}\right)\right)\,. \end{align}\]

Where:

  • $\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}_{n}$: Sharpe ratio of trial $n$.
  • $\mu$: Mean of the Sharpe ratios of the trials.
  • $\sigma$: Dispersion of the Sharpe ratios of the trials, their standard deviation.
  • $\gamma$: Euler-Mascheroni constant, about $0.5772$.
  • $\Phi$: Distribution function of the standard normal distribution, and $\Phi^{-1}$ its quantile function.
  • $N$: Number of independent trials of the search. It can be fractional, and it is at least one.
  • $e$: Euler's number.

The maximum of $N \geq 1$ draws is at least one draw, so its expectation is at least $\mu$. The floor at zero keeps that bound. The source states the approximation for $N \gg 1$ and has no floor. Without the floor, the approximation falls below $\mu$ for $N < 1.2836$, and it is $-\infty$ at $N = 1$. For one trial, or for trials with no dispersion, the expected maximum is $\mu$.

Arguments

  • N: Number of independent trials. A fractional value is an effective number of trials.
  • sigma: Standard deviation of the Sharpe ratios of the trials.
  • mu: Mean of the Sharpe ratios of the trials.

Validation

  • isfinite(N) and N >= 1.
  • isfinite(sigma) and sigma >= 0.
  • isfinite(mu).

Returns

  • sr::Number: The approximate expected maximum Sharpe ratio, in the frequency of sigma and mu.

rk, rt, rr = expected_risk_ret_ratio(res.r, res.ret, res.w, res.pr; sca = res.sca, rf = rf)

A result stores res.r and res.ret resolved. So this call gives the aggregates that the optimisation optimised, for one item or for many items on each axis. A caller can also name the measures by hand. Then the figure is equal to that of the optimisation only when the caller names the same measures and the same scalariser. The same holds for fees and rf.

A NonRiskJuMPOptimisationResult has no r, because its solve resolved no measure. With such a result, the caller must name a measure.

Compute the Sharpe ratio information criterion (SRIC) of a portfolio.

The SRIC corrects the risk-adjusted return ratio for the estimation error of an optimisation, which makes the in-sample ratio too large. expected_sric subtracts from the ratio a penalty that reads the number of assets and the number of observations.

Mathematical definition

\[\begin{align} \mathrm{SRIC}(\boldsymbol{w}) &= \mathrm{ratio}(\boldsymbol{w}) - P\left(\mathrm{ratio}(\boldsymbol{w})\right)\,. \end{align}\]

Where:

  • $\mathrm{SRIC}(\boldsymbol{w})$: Sharpe ratio information criterion.
  • $\boldsymbol{w}$: Portfolio weights vector $N \times 1$.
  • $\mathrm{ratio}(\boldsymbol{w})$: Risk-adjusted return ratio of expected_ratio, which states its own closed form.
  • $P$: Estimation-error penalty of sric_penalty, which states its own closed form.

Multiplicity

The function penalises the ratio of expected_ratio, so the rule is the same as for that function. The ratio divides one aggregate by the other. sca scalarises the risk axis, and the return axis sums at the settings.scale weights of its terms. The function subtracts the penalty once from the scalar ratio, and not from each item.

  • r: Risk measure, or a vector of them.
  • ret: Return estimator, or a vector of them.

  • pr: Prior result.

  • sca: Scalariser that reduces a vector r to one number. It has no effect on a single measure.
  • strict: If true, weights that hold an asset outside the Investable Mask raise an ArgumentError that names the assets. If false, a warning names the assets, and the function drops their weights. The reduction also sets the number of assets of the penalty to the number of assets that the prior can estimate.

A concrete element type, through concrete_typed_array_if_abstract, is an example.

The scalar method:

  1. Return a with no change, and drop args... and kwargs.... This method is the leaf of the recursion. Because of it, a call of the verb on a type with no tags returns the value and raises no error.

The vector method:

PortfolioOptimisers.brinson_attribution — Function
brinson_attribution(X::TimeArray, w::VecNum, wb::VecNum,
                    asset_classes::DataFrame, col, date0 = nothing, date1 = nothing)

Compute the Brinson performance attribution of each asset class [3].

brinson_attribution returns a DataFrame with the Brinson attribution of each asset class. It splits the total excess return of the class into asset allocation, security selection and interaction. The function reads the asset weights, the benchmark weights and the class of each asset over the date range.

date0 and date1 are positional arguments after col. The filter applies only when the call gives both. A call that gives only one of them attributes the whole of X.

X holds prices, not returns. The period return of an asset is the ratio of its last value in the range to its first value, minus one. This ratio is a return only when the entries are prices.

The function attributes one evaluation period, between the two dates, and it reads no weight path. w and wb stay the same over that period. So the function attributes a fund whose holdings drifted in the period at its weights at the start. A multi-period attribution that follows the drift needs a rule that links the effects of the single periods into a cumulative effect. This library has no such rule, so it has no such method. Attribute each period alone, and link the results outside the library.

Both class returns divide by the weight of the class, and no guard stops a division by zero. A class with zero portfolio weight makes its $r_{i}$ a NaN. A class with zero benchmark weight makes its $r_{i}^{b}$ a NaN. The two cases spread differently, because $\mathrm{AA}_{i}$ reads only $r_{i}^{b}$. A zero portfolio weight keeps $\mathrm{AA}_{i}$ finite, and it makes the other three rows of that class a NaN. A zero benchmark weight makes all four rows a NaN.

The Total column is a row sum, so its entry of a row is a NaN when one of the class entries of the row is a NaN. The columns of the other classes stay finite, because each column reads only its own class and $r^{b}$. Do not remove the rows of the empty class from asset_classes alone. Then asset_classes and w have different lengths, and the function raises a DimensionMismatch.

Mathematical definition

\[\begin{align} r_{j} &= \dfrac{P_{j,\,1}}{P_{j,\,0}} - 1\\ r^{b} &= \sum_{j} w_{j}^{b}\, r_{j}\\ r_{i} &= \dfrac{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{C}_{i}} w_{j}\, r_{j}}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{C}_{i}} w_{j}}\\ r_{i}^{b} &= \dfrac{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{C}_{i}} w_{j}^{b}\, r_{j}}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{C}_{i}} w_{j}^{b}}\\ \mathrm{AA}_{i} &= \left(w_{i} - w_{i}^{b}\right) \left(r_{i}^{b} - r^{b}\right)\\ \mathrm{SS}_{i} &= w_{i}^{b} \left(r_{i} - r_{i}^{b}\right)\\ \mathrm{I}_{i} &= \left(w_{i} - w_{i}^{b}\right) \left(r_{i} - r_{i}^{b}\right)\\ \mathrm{TER}_{i} &= \mathrm{AA}_{i} + \mathrm{SS}_{i} + \mathrm{I}_{i}\,. \end{align}\]

Where:

  • $\mathcal{C}_{i}$: The $i$-th asset class, the set of assets whose col entry is that class.
  • $P_{j,\,0}$, $P_{j,\,1}$: Price of asset $j$ at the first and at the last observation of the range.
  • $r_{j}$: Return of asset $j$ over the range.
  • $w_{j}$, $w_{j}^{b}$: Portfolio and benchmark weight of asset $j$.
  • $w_{i} = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{C}_{i}} w_{j}$, $w_{i}^{b} = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{C}_{i}} w_{j}^{b}$: Portfolio and benchmark weight of the class.
  • $r_{i}$, $r_{i}^{b}$: Portfolio and benchmark return of the class. Each is the weighted sum of the returns of its members, divided by the weight of the class. So a class return is a weighted average and not a weighted sum.
  • $r^{b}$: Benchmark return of the whole universe.
  • $\mathrm{AA}_{i}$: Asset allocation of the class, the part of the excess return that comes from the weight of the class.
  • $\mathrm{SS}_{i}$: Security selection of the class, the part that comes from the choice of assets in the class.
  • $\mathrm{I}_{i}$: Interaction of the class, the product of the two differences.
  • $\mathrm{TER}_{i}$: Total excess return of the class, the sum of the three.

Algorithm

  1. Filter the date range. When the call gives date0 and date1, find the first and the last observation of X in the range. These are idx1 and idx2. Otherwise take the whole of X, so idx1 is 1 and idx2 is length(X).
  2. Make ret, the period return of each asset, from the values at idx1 and idx2.
  3. Take the dot product of ret and wb. This is ret_b, the benchmark return of the whole universe.
  4. Read the class of each asset from column col of asset_classes. Build sets_mat, the membership matrix. Its entry is true when the asset belongs to the class.
  5. Take the next class. Sum the two weight vectors over its members, to get w_i and wb_i. Divide the two weighted sums of the returns of its members by those weights, to get ret_i and ret_b_i.
  6. Compute AA_i, SS_i, I_i and TER_i of that class. Write the four numbers as the column of the class in df.
  7. Repeat steps 5 and 6 for the other classes.
  8. Add the Total column, the sum of the class columns for each row.

Arguments

  • X: TimeArray of asset prices.
  • w: Vector of portfolio weights.
  • wb: Vector of benchmark weights.
  • asset_classes: DataFrame that gives the class of each asset.
  • col: Column of asset_classes that holds the class of each asset.
  • date0: Optional start date of the attribution period.
  • date1: Optional end date of the attribution period.

Returns

  • df::DataFrame: DataFrame with one row each for asset allocation, security selection, interaction and the total effect. It has one column for each asset class, and a Total column.
